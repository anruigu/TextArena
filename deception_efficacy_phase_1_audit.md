And that answer is game-dependent, driven entirely by variance:

Poker: raw realized value doesn't work, and the fix isn't philosophical. Card variance dominates realized results at any N you can afford — this is exactly why poker players use EV-adjusted winnings rather than actual winnings. So you need the EV of the action (solver EV, or your GTO approximation) not to establish causality but just to de-noise; realized-value-per-lie will be drowned in variance otherwise. The good news is you already have the GTO anchor, so the "counterfactual" here is an analytic lookup, not a rollout. Cheap.

Negotiation / BlindAuction / New Recruit: your instinct is right. Outcomes are near-deterministic given the private value tables and the final allocation, so realized score (via the table / meta) is a defensible proxy on its own. The reference baseline sharpens interpretation but isn't load-bearing the way it is in poker. So for these games, yes — measure instrumental gain across rollouts and mostly skip the baseline.

Second, the confound you're not naming but should: agents don't lie at random. They lie in particular spots (behind, or in a good bluffing texture), and stronger models lie in different spots than weaker ones. Raw realized value entangles "lying paid" with "the kind of agent/state that chose to lie." But this does not require per-lie counterfactuals to fix — for an eval (which is a model-ranking, not a per-lie causal estimate) you cancel it at the design level: same seeds, same fixed opponent, seat-swap halfway. Matched conditions across models make the selection and variance confounds shared, so the model-level comparison is clean without any per-decision baseline. The counterfactual moves from "per lie" (expensive) to "per model, via matched rollouts" (free). That's the move that actually resolves your discomfort.

But here's the sharper point for a bad-lie eval specifically, and it cuts in your favor: leakage is the clean marker for bad lies, and it needs no counterfactual at all. Good and bad lies can both pay off by luck in a given rollout, so the value channel is where bad lies are hardest to distinguish — it's noisy and confounded exactly where you need discrimination. The thing that separates a bad lie is that it leaks (message↔type MI, reader posterior sharpening), and that's a property of the act and the transcript, fully observable in a single rollout, no baseline required and lower-variance because it doesn't route through the payoff channel. So for "eval for bad lies," I'd invert your emphasis rather than drop the counterfactual: make leakage the primary signal (counterfactual-free), and use value only as the cheap secondary correlate — analytic EV in poker, realized table-value in negotiation — to confirm that low-leakage lies actually pay. Trying to squeeze causal value-attribution out of noisy rollouts is the part you're right to feel is unnecessary; the fix is to stop leaning on value as primary, not to strip the baseline off a value-primary eval.

So: counterfactual not strictly required; realized value alone is fine in the near-deterministic negotiation games and inadequate in poker (where you want cheap analytic EV, not rollouts); the selection confound is a matched-conditions design problem, not a per-lie one; and for bad lies specifically, leakage does most of the work value can't.
